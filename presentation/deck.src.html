02PROBLEM

The gap

Telemetry has no word for "he sounds done"

Every input a race engineer trusts is numeric. Tyre temps, fuel delta, sector times — all of it plotted, thresholded, alarmed. The driver's radio is the one channel that carries state no sensor measures: confusion, fatigue, the edge in the voice two laps before the mistake.

It's also the one channel nobody processes. It arrives as audio, mid-race, when the engineer is busy. It gets heard once, in the moment, by someone with no attention to spare — and then it's gone. The signal exists. It just never becomes data.

What the brief asked for

  • Upload or play a driver radio clip
  • Speech converted to readable text
  • Tone analysed — calm, stressed or tired
  • Shown next to lap times, so stress lines up with slower laps
Say this

"Open with the asymmetry. An F1 team measures everything about the car and nothing about the driver's state — except the one channel that's full of it, which they throw away. We're not adding a sensor. We're reading the one they already have."

03DEMO

Live, on real race data

Melbourne 2024, Lando Norris, 22 radio calls

No sample files, no staged clips. Race Radio pulls the session list, driver list, lap times and team radio straight from OpenF1, then pulls the actual broadcast audio from Formula 1's own livetiming host. Pick a year, a race, a driver — the picker sorts drivers by how many radio calls exist, so you never land on one with none.

Hit Transcribe & analyse and the clip goes through the same pipeline as any other audio in the system. The result comes back over a websocket: the words, a tone read, and a coloured bar on the lap chart at the lap that call landed on.

{{IMG:race-radio-viewport|Race Radio after five of Norris's calls were analysed. Bars are lap times; colour is the tone read on the call nearest that lap.}}

Two transcripts from that run, verbatim: "It sounded like as per P2 at the minute. Understood. As per P2." and "Bueno, sí, gracias." — the second one matters: the ASR is multilingual and auto-detects, which team radio needs badly.

And the honest read of this run. Five calls, four laps. Two came back stressed, on laps 7 and 8 — and the lap times there were 1:22.744 and 1:22.630, against 1:22.632 on the calm lap before them. No slowdown. On a five-call sample the stress read did not predict pace, and we're showing you that rather than cropping it.

That's the instrument working correctly: it surfaced a signal, the signal was checkable, and this time it didn't pay off. The question the brief poses only becomes answerable across a season of calls — which is precisely what nobody can do by ear, and what this makes possible.

04PIPELINE

Under the hood

Nine stages, every one resumable

A clip isn't "sent to an API". It moves through nine named stages, each writing its own rows and emitting its own progress event. If one rejects — too short, no speech, corrupt container — it fails with a reason code, not a stack trace.

1 VALIDATING 2 PREPROCESSING 3 TRANSCRIBING 4 DIARIZING 5 RECONCILING 6 EMBEDDING 7 IDENTIFYING 8 POSTPROCESSING 9 INDEXING

What each one actually does

PREPROCESSINGHigh-pass at 70 Hz, spectral denoise, EBU R128 loudness normalise to −23 LUFS, then voice-activity detection. Radio audio is compressed, clipped and noisy — this is why the transcript is readable at all.
TRANSCRIBINGWhisper large-v3-turbo with word-level timestamps and per-token logprobs. Language auto-detected.
DIARIZINGpyannote 3.1 splits the audio by speaker — driver vs. engineer on the same channel.
RECONCILINGSnaps speaker turns to word boundaries, so a speaker change never lands mid-word.
IDENTIFYINGECAPA voice embeddings matched against enrolled profiles by cosine similarity, with a margin test so a near-tie returns "unknown" instead of guessing.
INDEXINGEvery utterance embedded into pgvector for semantic search across the whole archive.
Say this

"The preprocessing stage is the unglamorous one that makes the rest work. Team radio is heavily compressed and clipped. Feed it to Whisper raw and you get mush. Normalise the loudness and gate the silence first and you get sentences."

05TONE

The stress read — and its honest limits

Calibrated per driver, not against a global threshold

Tone comes from four acoustic features: speech rate, mean pitch, pitch variability and voiced ratio. Rate and F0 are the two features most consistently tied to vocal stress in the speech literature, and both survive a noisy radio channel better than spectral detail does.

The part we're proud of is the baseline. A fixed pitch cutoff is meaningless across drivers — a naturally high voice would read permanently stressed. So each driver gets a rolling baseline built from their own calm calls in that session, an exponential moving average that resists a single noisy sample. After three calm samples, the classifier switches from absolute thresholds to relative to this driver, today: 45% faster than their own norm reads stressed, 45% slower with flattened pitch reads tired.

We are not going to oversell this. It's a transparent heuristic over interpretable features — not a trained speech-emotion model. It gives a directional read and an auditable reason, and every threshold is visible and tunable in the UI. Swapping in a trained SER model is a registry entry and a stage edit; the plumbing around it is the hard part, and that part is done.

Say this

"If a judge asks 'is this real emotion detection?' — answer it head-on. It's a heuristic on rate and pitch, calibrated per driver. That honesty is a feature: the thresholds are on screen and adjustable, so an engineer can tune it to their driver instead of trusting a black box."

07OPERABLE

Built to be run by someone who isn't us

Every threshold on screen. No redeploy.

Roughly forty pipeline parameters are editable live and apply to the very next job — denoise strength, VAD padding, speaker-count bounds, identification thresholds, quality grade cutoffs. A demo where the judge can move a slider and re-run beats a demo where we swear it's configurable.

Models are swappable from the same page. Six Whisper sizes, two diarization pipelines, two embedding models — download on demand with live progress, then activate. FastAPI in front, an arq worker behind it, Postgres with pgvector for embeddings and Redis for the queue. Jobs are retried with attempt limits; every run records its stage timings, so a slow clip tells you which stage was slow.

The whole thing runs fully offline — HF_HUB_OFFLINE=1 is enforced at runtime, models are resolved from local disk, and the only network call in the entire application is the F1 data fetch itself. Audio never leaves the machine.

09LIMITS

What's real, what isn't, yet

The parts we'd defend, and the parts we'd fix

Real, demonstrated live

  • End-to-end transcription, diarization and speaker ID on real F1 team radio
  • Tone read pinned to the nearest lap, colouring the lap chart
  • Per-driver baseline calibration across a session
  • Fully offline inference; live model swap and pipeline tuning from the UI

Honest gaps

  • Tone is a heuristic, not a trained classifier — directional, not diagnostic.
  • No validation set. We have no labelled stress corpus for F1 radio, so we report no accuracy number rather than an invented one.
  • No correlation demonstrated yet. In our five-call sample the stressed calls did not precede slower laps. We're reporting the null result, not hiding it.
  • The baseline is per worker process. Restart the worker and a driver's calibration starts over, so early calls in a session are judged on absolute thresholds until three calm samples land.
  • Lap pinning is nearest-timestamp, which is right for a call made on track and loose for one made in the pit lane.
  • CPU inference is ~2.5× realtime per clip — fine for post-session review, not yet for live race engineering. The device toggle is there for the GPU box.
Say this

"Say this slide out loud rather than hiding it. Judges trust a team that names its own weaknesses more than one that claims 94% accuracy on a number they can't source."

10CLOSE

Where it goes

From post-session review to the pit wall

The natural next step is the one the architecture already anticipates: the Live mode and the Race Radio mode are the same engine. Point the live ingest at a session's radio feed instead of a microphone and the stress read becomes a real-time strip alongside the timing tower — the engineer sees the driver's state on the same screen as the lap delta, while there's still a race left to change.

Beyond that: a trained speech-emotion model dropped into the existing registry, stress trends tracked across a season per driver, and a correlation view that answers the question this brief is really asking — does a stressed call actually precede a slower lap? We built the instrument that can finally measure it.

The driver has been telling the team how it's going all along. Now somebody's listening.

Close on

"Every team already records this audio. Nobody reads it. We turned the one channel that carries the driver's state into the one thing the pit wall understands — a number on a chart, next to the lap time."
